## 1. Executive summary

- **Recommended core: IWDA 70% / EIMI 10% / AGGU 20%** — iShares Core MSCI World (developed equity), iShares Core MSCI EM IMI (emerging equity), iShares Core Global Aggregate Bond USD-Hedged (stabiliser). All three Irish-domiciled, all accumulating, all tradeable in USD on the LSE. Blended TER **0.178%**.
- **The trade-off you are accepting:** roughly 12 bp/yr of headline cost versus the US-listed mirror (VT + BNDW at 0.058%), in exchange for avoiding investor-level US dividend withholding and US-situs estate-tax exposure as a non-US person with no US–Brazil income tax treaty. For a Brazilian resident the withholding arithmetic normally outweighs the fee gap — but it depends on the rate that actually applies to you, and that is a question for your accountant, not a number I will assert.
- **The third ticker is a choice, not a requirement.** IWDA + EIMI exists so that *you* set the emerging-markets weight. If you do not want to make that call, collapse them into one fund — **VWRA** (FTSE All-World, 0.19%) or **SPYI/IMID** (MSCI ACWI IMI, 0.17%, includes small caps) — and hold two tickers instead of three. The cost difference is under 1 bp/yr. I lead with three because EM weighting is the one place in a global portfolio where market weight and defensible judgement genuinely diverge, and 25 years is the horizon over which that divergence can matter.
- **Expected downside:** roughly **-37% to -40%** peak-to-trough in a 2008-style equity crisis; roughly **-15% to -18%** in a 2022-style inflationary shock where both sleeves fall together and the bond allocation cushions almost nothing. Plan around the second scenario, not the first — it is the one that surprises people.
- **A verification catch worth flagging:** justETF currently lists 0.14% TER for both Vanguard FTSE All-World UCITS share classes. Vanguard's own factsheet dated 30 June 2026 states an OCF of **0.19%**. I have used the issuer figure throughout. Do not screen this fund family on aggregator data.

## 4. Selected ETFs — detailed notes

### IWDA / SWDA — iShares Core MSCI World UCITS ETF (IE00B4L5Y983)

**Overview.** Tracks MSCI World (Net) — 23 developed countries, roughly the top 85% of listed float in each. 1,283 holdings. Physical, optimised sampling. Accumulating. Launched September 2009, so a genuine 16-year track record rather than a backtest.

**Portfolio characteristics.** Sector: Information Technology 29.48%, Financials 16.54%, Industrials 11.49%, Consumer Discretionary 8.95%, Health Care 8.84%. Top 10 holdings 25.69% of the fund — NVIDIA 5.17%, Apple 4.76%, Microsoft 2.95%, Amazon 2.58%, Alphabet A+C 4.16% combined. P/E 26.09, P/B 4.08. Three-year standard deviation 12.42%, beta to benchmark 1.00. Geographic breakdown `n/v` from the issuer factsheet, but it is necessarily more US-concentrated than the 61.7% US weight of the all-world FTSE index, since excluding EM raises every developed weight proportionally.

**Costs and tracking.** TER 0.20%. Tracking difference is where this fund earns its keep: annualised share-class NAV return versus MSCI World Net was **+0.01 pp at 1y, +0.04 pp at 3y, +0.06 pp at 5y** — the fund has beaten its net benchmark, which is what securities lending plus optimised sampling plus recoverable withholding can do. Securities lending return 0.02% for the year to 30 June 2026, average 8.08% of AUM on loan, collateralisation 108%. Fund retains 62.5% of lending income.

**Liquidity and IBKR implementation.** Nine listings. Use **IWDA on the LSE in USD** (SEDOL B4L5Y98) to keep the account in one currency and avoid an unnecessary FX leg. SWDA (LSE, GBP) and EUNL (Xetra, EUR) are the same fund in other currencies — do not mix them across purchases or your average-cost records become a mess. Limit orders during the London session; avoid the first and last ten minutes. Retail-sized orders need no working strategy.

**Risks.** Market risk first. Concentration is the live issue: a single stock at 5.17% and a top-10 at 25.7% is high by historical standards, and the technology weight near 30% is a sector bet you are taking passively. Currency: unhedged, so returns are in local currencies translated to USD and then to BRL — two layers. Structural: physical optimised (not full replication), so a small sampling error is possible; no counterparty exposure beyond securities lending, which is collateralised above 100%.

**Role.** Core equity engine. Diversifies across 23 developed markets. Overlaps with nothing else in the proposed portfolio — by MSCI construction it shares zero constituents with EIMI.

### EIMI — iShares Core MSCI EM IMI UCITS ETF (IE00BKM4GZ66)

**Overview.** Tracks MSCI Emerging Markets Investable Market Index (Net) — large, mid **and** small cap across emerging markets. 3,056 holdings. Physical, fully replicated. Accumulating. Launched May 2014.

**Portfolio characteristics.** Sector: Information Technology 37.49%, Financials 19.28%, Consumer Discretionary 8.93%, Industrials 7.81%, Materials 6.27%. P/E 18.79, P/B 2.43 — a real valuation discount to IWDA's 26.09 and 4.08, which is the substance of the EM case and also its warning. Three-year standard deviation 17.46% against IWDA's 12.42%: EM is roughly 40% more volatile, and sizing should respect that.

**Costs and tracking.** TER 0.18%, cheaper than the developed-market fund, which is unusual and reflects scale. Securities lending return 0.12% for the year to 30 June 2026 — six times IWDA's, with 11.82% average on loan; in a fund with a 0.18% fee, lending income is materially offsetting the expense ratio. Explicit tracking-difference figures were not published in text form on the product page; three-year beta to benchmark is 0.999.

**Liquidity and IBKR implementation.** Ten listings. Use **EIMI on the LSE in USD** (SEDOL BKM4GZ6). EMIM on the LSE is the GBP line of the same fund — a common and expensive confusion. Spreads are wider than IWDA's, particularly outside the overlap when Asian markets are closed; place limits mid-session.

**Risks.** Higher market and political risk by construction. Currency risk is larger and less diversifiable than in developed markets. Country concentration in China, Taiwan, India and Korea. Liquidity of underlying small caps is the weakest link in a stress event. Full replication removes sampling risk but means holding the tail of genuinely illiquid names.

**Role.** Emerging-markets sleeve, held separately precisely so the weight is a decision rather than an accident. **Note the Korea classification difference:** MSCI treats South Korea as emerging, so EIMI holds it. FTSE treats Korea as developed — in the FTSE Global All Cap data, Korea (2.8%) sits under Pacific, not under the 10.0% emerging-markets subtotal. Choosing the MSCI pair rather than a FTSE all-world fund moves roughly 3% of global equity between your "developed" and "emerging" buckets. It is not a technicality if you are setting EM weight deliberately.

### AGGU — iShares Core Global Aggregate Bond UCITS ETF USD Hedged (Acc) (IE00BZ043R46)

**Overview.** Tracks the Bloomberg Global Aggregate Bond Index — global investment-grade government, government-related, corporate and securitised debt, hedged to USD. 19,977 holdings. Physical, sampled. Accumulating. Launched November 2017.

**Portfolio characteristics.** Effective duration **6.09**. Weighted average maturity 8.07 years. Weighted average YTM **4.00%**, weighted average coupon 3.05%. Sector: Treasuries 53.16%, agency fixed rate 9.56%, owned-no-guarantee 6.33%, banking 4.82%, local authority 3.12%, supranational 2.82%. Investment grade throughout. Three-year standard deviation 4.12%; three-year beta 0.563 — this behaves like a bond fund, not an equity proxy.

**Costs and tracking.** TER 0.10%. Securities lending return 0.03%. Currency hedging is executed at share-class level with monthly rebalancing; hedging cost is embedded in returns rather than the TER, and in a steep-curve environment it is not trivial — judge this fund on total return versus a USD aggregate, not on its TER.

**Liquidity and IBKR implementation.** Listed on the LSE in USD, SIX in USD, plus Mexico and Tel Aviv. Use the **LSE USD line (AGGU)**. The share class holds $4.08bn within a $14.10bn fund, so the specific line is well supported. Limit orders, London session. Note there are nine share classes of this fund including unhedged and distributing variants — confirm the ISIN, not just the ticker, before your first order.

**Risks.** Duration risk is the headline: a 100 bp parallel rise in global yields costs roughly 6% of price, offset over time by the 4.00% carry. Credit risk is investment-grade only but not zero. Hedging risk: the USD hedge removes foreign-currency volatility but not BRL exposure, and hedged returns converge toward USD short rates plus the credit and term premium of the underlying. Structural: sampled replication across nearly 20,000 bonds, so tracking is approximate by design.

**Role.** Stabiliser and rebalancing reservoir. Diversifies against equity drawdowns driven by growth shocks. Fails to diversify against inflation shocks — that limitation is explicit in section 5 and is not solvable within an aggregate bond fund.

**I recommend Model B.** Twenty-five years is long enough that Model C's bond weight costs more in expected return than it buys in comfort, and short enough that Model A's 10% sleeve is too thin to fund meaningful rebalancing purchases in a crash. Twenty percent in AGGU is roughly five years of a typical drawdown-recovery cycle's worth of buying power, which is the actual function of the bond sleeve during accumulation — not volatility reduction for its own sake.

**Diversification.** Model B holds roughly 4,300 equity securities across 23 developed and all major emerging markets, plus about 20,000 bonds. Constituent overlap between IWDA and EIMI is **zero** — MSCI assigns each country to exactly one of the two indices, so no company is double-counted. The gap in coverage is developed-market small caps: MSCI World captures about 85% of listed float per country, so roughly 15% of the developed market by capitalisation is absent. EIMI, being an IMI index, *does* include emerging small caps, which is a mild inconsistency in the pair. If that bothers you, SPYI (MSCI ACWI IMI, 4,978 holdings, 0.17%) fixes it in one ticker. Concentration is the more pressing issue than coverage: at 70% IWDA, the top 10 stocks are about **18% of your total portfolio**, and NVIDIA alone is about **3.6%**.

**Drawdown behaviour.** Three scenarios, modelled from the verified sleeve statistics rather than recalled history:

- *2008-style growth and credit shock.* Global equities down roughly 50%, global aggregate up mid-single digits on flight-to-quality: Model B loses about **-38%** peak to trough. The bond sleeve offsets perhaps two percentage points of that and, more usefully, gives you something to sell into equities near the bottom.
- *2020-style fast crash.* Equities down about 35% over weeks, bonds slightly positive: roughly **-27%**, recovering fast enough that the main risk is behavioural rather than financial.
- *2022-style inflationary shock.* Equities down about 20% and a duration-6 bond sleeve down about 10% simultaneously: roughly **-18%**, with the stabiliser contributing nothing. This is the scenario people are unprepared for, and no allocation within these three funds solves it. If it becomes a live concern, the fix is a short-duration or inflation-linked sleeve — a fourth ticker, with the operational cost that implies. I am not proposing it now.

**Long-term suitability.** The structure survives 25 years because none of the three funds requires a view to keep working. Rebalance **with new contributions**, not sales: direct each monthly purchase to whichever sleeve is furthest below target. Use bands rather than a calendar — ±5 pp on the bond sleeve, ±3 pp on EM — and only sell if contributions cannot close the gap within a few months. What would justify a change: an issuer fee rise, sustained tracking deterioration, a fund falling below roughly $1bn, or your horizon shortening below about ten years (at which point the bond weight should start rising and the distributing share classes become interesting again). What would *not* justify a change: one to three years of EM underperformance, a change in your macro view, or a cheaper competitor launching with two years of history.

**Complexity cost.** Three tickers, one trading currency (USD), one exchange (LSE), zero distribution events because all three accumulate. That is three positions to track and three BRL cost-basis records. Compare honestly:

- **Two-ticker alternative:** VWRA 80 / AGGU 20 = blended TER 0.172%, or SPYI 80 / AGGU 20 = **0.156%**. Both are *cheaper* than Model B. SPYI additionally adds developed small caps. What you give up is the ability to set the EM weight, and you accept SPYI's smaller $8.2bn base and a measured 10-year tracking difference of about -0.11 pp/yr against its index (its 5-year figure is +0.18 pp/yr, so this is noise-adjacent, but it is not IWDA's record). **The three-ticker version does not save money — it buys control. If you will not actually use the control, take two tickers.**
- **US-listed mirror:** VT 80 / BNDW 20 = blended TER **0.058%**, about 12 bp/yr cheaper than Model B, which compounds to roughly 3% of terminal wealth over 25 years. Against that: VT distributes quarterly and BNDW monthly — that is on the order of 16 distribution events a year to convert and record in BRL — and, as a non-US person in a country with no income tax treaty with the United States, investor-level withholding applies to the full distribution. On a ~1.5% equity dividend yield, that drag is a multiple of the 12 bp fee saving. Add US-situs estate-tax exposure on US-domiciled holdings. **Run the arithmetic with your accountant using the withholding rate that actually applies to you; my expectation is that the Irish structure wins on after-tax return, but the input is his, not mine.**

## 6. Brazil tax and operational considerations

Structural observations only. Nothing here is a rate, a threshold or a filing instruction.

**Domicile is the largest single decision in this proposal.** All three recommended funds are Irish UCITS. Two distinct mechanisms are in play and they are frequently conflated: (i) withholding suffered *inside* the fund on the dividends its underlying holdings pay, which for an Irish fund holding US equities is reduced by the Ireland–US treaty, and (ii) withholding on the distribution *from* the fund to you, which does not arise on an accumulating Irish fund but does arise on a US-domiciled ETF paying a Brazilian resident, and Brazil has no income tax treaty with the United States. A third, separate issue is US estate tax on US-situs assets held by a non-resident alien, which is not a function of income at all and becomes more consequential as the portfolio grows.

**All three funds accumulate, which changes the shape of your reporting rather than eliminating it.** There are no dividend events to convert to BRL, but the accrual of income inside the fund and the treatment of accumulated gains on eventual disposal are exactly the points to confirm — the direction of the effect depends on rules you should verify rather than rules I should assert.

**FX and BRL conversion points.** Under Model B you have three purchase events per contribution cycle at most, all in USD on one exchange, and zero distribution events. That is close to the minimum achievable record-keeping load for a diversified global portfolio. Convert BRL to USD deliberately in blocks through IBKR's FX rather than letting trades convert implicitly — it is cheaper and it produces one clean conversion record per block instead of one per trade.

**Realised versus unrealised.** Contribution-based rebalancing keeps the portfolio buy-and-hold and defers realisation across the whole accumulation phase. This is available to you because you are still contributing, and it is the single largest operational reason to prefer bands-plus-contributions over calendar rebalancing with sales.

**Character of return.** Model B shifts a fifth of the portfolio into a sleeve whose return is predominantly interest income accruing at roughly 4.00% YTM rather than capital appreciation. Whether that accrual is treated differently from equity appreciation in your reporting is worth an explicit question.

These are structural observations, not tax advice. Confirm the specifics with your accountant or a current Brazilian tax reference before acting.
